The 7 Best RPG Habit Trackers for 2027

Nov 13, 2026 · 12 min read · by Kevin Castaneda

If you're reading this in the run-up to January, you probably know how the usual plan goes. A clean checklist app, two good weeks, one bad one, and the app moves to the last page of your phone. An RPG habit tracker is a different bet: your real habits become quests, the work earns experience points, and there's a character on screen that only grows when you do.

The category changed more in 2026 than in the five years before it. Two serious new games arrived (Levla and MainQuest), the older apps kept shipping, and one of the originals, EpicWin, no longer shows up on the App Store. So this is a new list, not last year's list with the date changed. Seven apps, what each one costs, where it runs, and the thing I'd check first: what a missed day costs you.

A quick disclosure: one of the seven, Gamified Lives, is the app I build. I've described the other six the way I'd want mine described, by what they do best, and where another app is the better fit I've said so.

The short version

  • The most game: Levla
  • If you quit after a missed day: Gamified Lives
  • Designed for ADHD, built on focus timers: MainQuest
  • A fantasy world with guilds on iPhone: TaskHero
  • Android, offline, pay once: LifeUp
  • Pixel art on a small budget: Habicat

What counts as an RPG habit tracker?

A normal habit tracker records whether you did the thing. An RPG habit tracker turns the doing into a system: you earn experience points (XP) for finishing tasks, a character or avatar levels up, and you unlock gear, cosmetics or new areas as you go. You'll see the same idea sold as a life RPG, an RPG life tracker, a gamified habit tracker, or a "level up your life" app. They all mean the same thing: your real life is the game.

The appeal is easy to explain. Points and progress bars give you a reward today for an action whose real payoff is weeks away. The risk is just as easy to explain. A lot of these games run on loss aversion, the well-documented finding that a loss stings about twice as much as an equal gain. Build the whole engine around something you can lose in one bad day and the motivation can turn into anxiety. That's why the table below has a column most lists skip.

How I compared them

For each app I went through its current store listing and the maker's own site in November 2026 and wrote down four things: what it costs, where it runs, how deep the game goes, and what the app says happens when you miss a day. The last one gets its own column because it's the best predictor I know of whether you'll still be using the app in March. I've spent the most hands-on time in Habitica and Levla, and obviously in my own. Where a listing didn't answer a question, the table says so instead of guessing.

Habitica: the deep classic

Habitica launched as HabitRPG in 2013 and it's still the app most people picture when they hear "RPG habit tracker." Pixel avatar, gear, pets, parties, quests, challenges, and the largest community in the category. It's still actively updated (the latest iOS release landed this month), and its own listing says it "can be fully enjoyed for free." The optional subscription is $4.99 a month or $47.99 a year on the US App Store and mostly buys gems and extras, not the tracker itself.

The catch is the same thing that makes it powerful. There's a lot to set up, and new users often describe being overwhelmed before they've tracked anything. And the core loop punishes: miss your Dailies and your character loses health, and on a party boss quest your friends take damage too. For consistent people that's a real motivator. For the rest of us, a rough week can make the app feel like a place you're in trouble.

Pick it if you love deep systems, want a web version, and want other people to quest with.

Levla: the most game in the category

Levla is the new entrant everyone asked me about this year, and it has earned the attention. Every habit, to-do and daily reflection earns XP, coins and stamina. Stamina sends your hero on adventures of up to 12 hours, where you hunt monsters and clear dungeons for loot. There are 18 skills across combat, gathering, crafting and utility, gear for every slot, and as of the 1.1 update, guilds, a world map and a library of ready-made habits.

It's also gentler than its depth suggests. The listing is explicit: "A missed day never costs your character health or gear," and you can skip, snooze or check in late. The one thing that does reset is the Oath, a pact where you and a friend each keep one habit and share a streak. The free tier is generous (unlimited habits, to-dos and reflections, no ads), and the membership, from $5.99 a month, adds timers, members-only regions and shared lists without boosting XP.

The trade-off is where the fun lives. The reward for your habits is a second game, and in a slow week you earn less stamina, so you get less of it. Some people love having that game to come back to. Others find they're feeding it. I compared it head-to-head in Levla vs. Habitica vs. Gamified Lives.

Pick it if you want the most complete RPG and you like an adventure layer of its own.

Gamified Lives: built for the day after you miss

Here's mine, and the honest version of where it fits. Gamified Lives is built around one question the others treat as a side issue: what happens the day after you miss? The answer here is that nothing is taken from you. The home screen shows Time Played, a number that only goes up, in place of a streak that can hit zero. Come back within 24 hours of a miss and the Phoenix Bonus pays double XP. A Resilience Score tracks how reliably you bounce back, not how long you went without slipping. It's the opposite of the loop I argue against in the case against streaks.

The game sits on top of your real list, not beside it. You build your own hero (any skin, hair and eye color), then dress it and furnish its room with coins that only come from finished tasks. Each week you pick a boss for one area of your life, and every real task you complete is a hit. Tasks you prove with a photo or a timer land a critical hit. Skip a day and the boss heals a little. That's the whole penalty.

The newest piece is plans. As of version 1.2, picking a goal gets you the whole plan behind it: the habits, today's tasks and how to do each one, with ready-made plans for building muscle, saving money, sleep, reading, meditation and quitting a habit. Squads are free. Premium ($9.99 a month or $75.99 a year, with a 7-day trial) adds an AI coach that knows your goals and your logs, replies after each session, reviews your week, and can build a plan for a goal the catalog doesn't cover.

Where it loses: it's iPhone only, the community is a fraction of Habitica's, and if you want a world to explore, Levla and Habitica are simply more game. The coach is Premium, and Premium costs more per month than either of them.

Pick it if you've quit a habit app the morning after a miss, or you want the plan as well as the tracker.

MainQuest: designed for ADHD, built on focus

MainQuest launched in 2026 and describes itself as designed with ADHD in mind. It runs on iOS, Android and the web, it's free to download with no ads, and its center of gravity is the focus timer: five modes (classic, Pomodoro, Breadcrumbs, Flowmodoro and Infinite), with focused time turning into XP. Around that sit quests, habits, skills, an inventory and a shop, plus friends, parties and accountability pacts.

Its miss rule is the opposite of gentle, and it's upfront about it. Ignore your quests and your HP drops. Let it hit zero and, in the maker's words, "you're down until someone revives you." If you have friends in the app, that's a clever piece of social pressure. If you're playing solo, know that going in. A Pro subscription unlocks unlimited skills, habit chains, analytics, dungeons and parties, and boss battles are listed as coming soon. It's young, with only a handful of App Store ratings so far, so expect it to change quickly. If focus is your main struggle, my guide to ADHD habit trackers covers what else to look for.

Pick it if starting and staying on a task is the real problem, and you have a friend who'll play too.

TaskHero: a fantasy world for your to-do list

TaskHero drops your habits, tasks and goals into a fantasy world called Tasklandia. You gain XP, level up, upgrade your character's stats, cast spells and collect gold for gear. There are guilds and shared quests if you want company, focus timers on your lists, and one setting I wish more apps had: you choose the consequences for overdue tasks, so the game is as strict or as forgiving as you make it.

It's free on iPhone and iPad, with in-app purchases for gold and gems and a $4.99 PlusPass. It has been around for years, is still updated often (a new version landed the week I checked), and has a small but happy set of App Store reviews.

Pick it if you want classic RPG trappings (spells, gear, a guild) and want to set your own difficulty.

LifeUp: the build-your-own life RPG on Android

LifeUp is the outlier, in a good way. It's Android only, it's a single one-time purchase, and its listing promises "No ads. No subscriptions. Ever." It works fully offline, keeps your data on your device and doesn't need an account. Since 2019 it has collected more than 300,000 installs and a rating near 4.8.

What you get is closer to a toolkit than a finished adventure. You define your own skills and level them with XP, earn coins, and spend them in a shop you stock yourself with real-life rewards at prices you set. There are loot boxes with drop rates you choose, crafting recipes, custom achievements, a Pomodoro timer, and a World module for shared goals with friends. The cost of all that freedom is setup time: the game is whatever you design.

Pick it if you're on Android, dislike subscriptions, and enjoy building your own system.

Habicat: pixel art on a small budget

Habicat is a pixel-style habit RPG for iPhone, iPad and Mac. Daily check-ins earn EXP and gems, chests drop pixel accessories for your character, and a monthly 30-day Boss Challenge runs alongside your habits. It's much lighter than Habitica or Levla, which is the point. The learning curve is short and the art is charming.

It's free to start, and the paid tier is the cheapest subscription here: $2.99 a month, $14.99 a year, or a lifetime unlock. One thing to know: the chests are loot boxes, and the App Store labels them that way.

Pick it if you want something small, cute and cheap that still feels like a game.

What happened to EpicWin?

EpicWin was on last year's list as the nostalgia pick. When I checked in November 2026, its App Store page no longer loaded in the US or UK stores, so I've taken it off this one. If you loved it for the retro feel, Habicat's pixel art and TaskHero's fantasy world are the closest in spirit.

How to choose one for 2027

Match the app to how you fail, not to its feature list.

  • You quit when apps get complicated. Skip Habitica and LifeUp. Start with Habicat, TaskHero or Gamified Lives.
  • You quit the day after you miss. Read the last column of the table again. Levla and Gamified Lives take nothing away. Habitica and MainQuest take HP.
  • You can't get started on a task. MainQuest's focus timers are the differentiator.
  • You don't know what to do each day, only what you want. Pick an app that gives you the plan as well as the checkbox. That's what Gamified Lives' plans are for.
  • You want other people. Habitica's community is the largest by a distance, and Levla now has guilds.
  • You're on Android and dislike subscriptions. LifeUp.

One more thing if you're setting this up for January. Most resolutions don't end on New Year's Day. They end on the first bad week, when the plan meets real life and the app starts to feel like a record of failure. I wrote about that in why resolutions fail. Frequently asked questions

What is the best RPG habit tracker for 2027?

It depends on what makes you quit. Habitica is the deepest classic and has the biggest community. Levla is the most complete game. MainQuest is designed for ADHD and built around focus timers. TaskHero offers a fantasy world with guilds on iPhone. LifeUp is the offline, pay-once pick on Android. Habicat is the low-cost pixel-art option. Gamified Lives is the pick if you tend to quit after a missed day, because nothing is taken from you when you miss.

Are there free RPG habit trackers?

Yes. Habitica, Levla, MainQuest, TaskHero, Habicat and Gamified Lives are all free to download and use, with optional paid tiers. Habitica's listing says it can be fully enjoyed for free, and Levla's free tier includes unlimited habits and to-dos with no ads. LifeUp is the exception: it is a one-time purchase on Android with no ads and no subscription.

What is the best RPG habit tracker for Android?

Habitica, Levla and MainQuest all run on Android. LifeUp is Android-first and works fully offline, with your data kept on your device and a single one-time purchase. Gamified Lives is iPhone only for now.

What is the best RPG habit tracker for ADHD?

MainQuest is the one designed for ADHD by name, with five focus-timer modes that earn XP. Whichever you choose, check what a missed day costs before you commit. Habitica and MainQuest take HP for missed tasks. Levla and Gamified Lives take nothing, and Gamified Lives pays bonus XP for coming back within 24 hours.

Is Habitica still worth using in 2027?

Yes, if you like deep systems and want a community. It is still updated, the core tracker is free, and the optional subscription is $4.99 a month or $47.99 a year on the US App Store. The trade-offs are the same as ever: setup takes effort, and missed Dailies cost your character health.

Is a life RPG app the same as an RPG habit tracker?

Yes. Life RPG, RPG life tracker, gamified habit tracker and level-up-your-life app all describe the same idea: your real habits and tasks are the quests, and your progress is a character or stat that levels up.

Do RPG habit trackers actually work?

For many people they do, because XP and visible progress give you an immediate reward for an action whose real payoff is weeks away. What matters most over months is how the app treats a missed day. An app that makes coming back easy keeps you longer than one that makes a single slip feel like failure.
