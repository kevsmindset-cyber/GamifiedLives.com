How to Make Your New Year's Resolution Stick This Year: An 8-Step Plan

Nov 18, 2026 · 11 min read · by Kevin Castaneda

You've probably made this resolution before. Same goal, same January, same quiet ending some time before Valentine's Day. So this isn't another list of reasons it goes wrong. I already wrote that one: why New Year's resolutions fail.

This is the other half. A plan you can set up in about twenty minutes, in order, with dates on it, for making the resolution actually happen in 2027. Everything in it works on paper. I make a habit app, and I'll say where it helps near the end, but you don't need it to do any of this.

The short version

  • One resolution, phrased as something to do.
  • A number and a date, divided into a weekly or daily amount.
  • A minimum version small enough for your worst day.
  • A time and a place, attached to something you already do.
  • A practice week before January 1.
  • A rule for the day you miss, written before you need it.
  • Ten minutes every Sunday to count and adjust.

First, the good news: making one is worth it

Resolutions have a reputation as a joke, so it's worth knowing what happens to people who make one compared with people who don't. A study in the Journal of Clinical Psychology followed 159 people who made a New Year's resolution and 123 who wanted to change something but made no resolution. Six months later, 46% of the resolvers were still succeeding. Among the people who hadn't made one, it was 4%.

So the resolution isn't the problem. Deciding, on a date, that you're going to change something is a real advantage. What decides whether you end up in the 46% is how the resolution is built, and that is what the eight steps are for.

Step 1: Pick one, and pick one you want

One resolution. Not a list. Every extra item is another thing to miss, and a list full of misses is a list you stop opening.

Then check that you want it, as opposed to feeling you should want it. Researchers at the University of Chicago tracked people's New Year's resolutions and found that enjoying the activity predicted who was still going. How important people said the goal was did not. People expected the opposite.

That doesn't mean only do fun things. It means choose the version of the goal you can stand. If you hate running, "get fit" doesn't have to mean running. Lift, swim, walk with a podcast, play pickup basketball. The version you half enjoy in March beats the best version you quit in January.

Step 2: Phrase it as something to do

A lot of resolutions start with "stop": stop eating junk, stop scrolling, stop wasting money. The trouble is that "stop" gives you nothing to do today, and no way to tell a good day from a lucky one.

A study of 1,066 people in Sweden followed their resolutions for a full year. Of those whose resolution was about starting something, 58.9% considered themselves successful at the end. Of those whose resolution was about avoiding or quitting something, it was 47.1%. So turn it around:

The "stop" versionThe "do" version
Stop eating junkCook dinner four nights a week
Stop scrolling at nightPhone charges in the kitchen, book goes on the pillow
Stop wasting moneyMove $84 to savings every Friday
Stop being so out of shapeThree workouts a week
Stop going to bed so lateLights out by 11 on weeknights

If the thing you're quitting is a real dependency, such as alcohol or nicotine, the "do" version still helps (what you'll do when the urge comes), but talk to a doctor as well. A blog post isn't the right tool for that part.

Step 3: Give it a number and a date, then divide

"Save more" can't be done on a Tuesday. "$1,000 by March 26" can, because it divides:

  • Save $1,000 in 12 weeks: 1,000 ÷ 12 is about $84 a week.
  • Read 12 books this year: at 300 pages each that's 3,600 pages, or about 10 pages a day.
  • Get back in shape: three workouts a week for 12 weeks is 36 workouts. Count them down.

The division is the whole trick. The yearly number is too big to feel, and the weekly number is small enough to do. Pick a first date that's 8 to 12 weeks out, even if the resolution is for the year. You can set the next stretch when you get there.

Step 4: Write a minimum version

Next to every task, write a version so small that a bad day can't beat it:

  • Ten pages becomes one page.
  • A full workout becomes a ten-minute walk.
  • $84 becomes $5.
  • Ten minutes of meditation becomes three slow breaths.

The rule that makes this work: on a bad day, the minimum counts in full. It isn't a consolation prize. Its job is to keep you someone who did the thing today, because the alternative isn't a full workout. The alternative is nothing, and then nothing again tomorrow. If that sounds familiar, it's the all-or-nothing trap, and the minimum version is the way out of it.

Step 5: Decide when and where

Most days you don't skip the task because you decided to. You skip it because the day never offered a moment and you never picked one. So pick it now, and attach it to something that already happens:

After I pour my coffee, I read ten pages at the kitchen table.
When I get home from work on Monday, Wednesday and Friday, I change straight into gym clothes.
When my paycheck lands, I move $84 before I do anything else.

Psychologists call these if-then plans, and they're one of the most tested ideas in goal research. A 2006 review pulled together 94 tests, and a 2024 update covered more than 600. The size of the benefit varies from study to study, but the direction is consistent: people who decide when and where follow through more often than people who only decide what.

Step 6: Do the first one before January 1

Here is a pattern I see a lot. Someone gets excited, buys the shoes, downloads the app, writes the plan, and feels finished. All of the energy went into getting ready. The next morning the plan is sitting there, complete, and nothing in it has been done. That's a heavy thing to wake up to.

So spend the last days of December on a practice week. From Saturday, December 26, do only the minimum version each day. One page. One walk. Five dollars. You'll find out the things you'd otherwise find out on January 2: the gym is closed on Sundays, the book is boring, the savings account needs two days to set up.

By New Year's Day you won't be starting. You'll be raising the number on something you've already done six times. A fresh date does give you a real push, which I covered in the earlier post, but it works better pointed at something that's already moving.

Step 7: Write your rule for the day you miss

You will miss a day. Probably in the first two weeks. What happens next is the only part that matters, so decide it now, while you're calm:

My rule: a missed day costs nothing. The next day I do the minimum version, whatever else is going on.

This isn't being soft on yourself. It's how habits form. In a University College London study, 96 volunteers each tried to build one new daily habit over 84 days. Missing a single day made no meaningful difference to whether the habit formed. Being inconsistent across the whole stretch did.

The same study is where the "66 days" figure comes from. That was the average time for a behavior to become automatic, and the range ran from 18 days to a projected 254. So if you start on January 1, the day it stops feeling like effort is closer to early March than to the end of January. If you're already past a miss and it has turned into a week, there's a step-by-step routine in how to restart a habit after missing days.

Step 8: Review it every Sunday

Ten minutes, once a week, three questions:

  1. What's the count? Workouts done, dollars moved, pages read. Just the number.
  2. What got in the way? One sentence. "Too tired after work" is useful. "I'm lazy" isn't.
  3. What changes this week? Move the time, shrink the task, or change the number.

If you planned $84 a week and managed $50, the plan was wrong, not you. Make it $60 and push the date back. A resolution you adjust in week three is still alive in week ten. One you refuse to adjust usually isn't.

If you can, tell one person the weekly number, and only that. Not the dream, the count. It's much harder to quietly drop something when a friend is going to ask "how many this week?"

Frequently asked questions

How do I make my New Year's resolution stick?

Pick one resolution, phrase it as something to do, and give it a number and a date. Divide that into a weekly or daily amount, write a minimum version for bad days, and attach it to something you already do. Start a few days before January 1, decide in advance what you'll do the day after you miss, and review it once a week.

Do New Year's resolutions actually work?

Better than not making one. A study in the Journal of Clinical Psychology followed 159 people who made a resolution and 123 who wanted to change something but didn't make one. Six months later, 46% of the resolvers were still succeeding, against 4% of the others.

How long does it take for a resolution to become a habit?

Longer than January. In a University College London study, a new daily behavior took 66 days on average to become automatic, and the range ran from 18 days to a projected 254. If you start on January 1, 2027, day 66 is Sunday, March 7. Plan for ten weeks, not three.

What is Quitter's Day, and when is it in 2027?

Quitter's Day is the second Friday of January, the day the fitness app Strava found people's New Year activity drops off. In 2027 it falls on Friday, January 8, one week after New Year's Day. Put your minimum version on the calendar for that day in advance.

How many New Year's resolutions should I make?

One. A second can wait until the first has survived a bad week. Every extra resolution is another thing to miss, and a pile of missed tasks is what makes people stop looking at the list at all.

What should I do if I've already broken my resolution?

Do the minimum version today. Not Monday, not next month. A missed day, or a missed week, doesn't erase the days you did. Then look at why it broke: if the task was too big, shrink it; if the time was wrong, move it.

Should I wait until January 1 to start?

No. Use the last week of December as a practice week and do the smallest version each day. January 1 then becomes the day you raise the number, which is much easier than the day you start from nothing.

Start this week, not on January 1

The people who keep a resolution aren't the ones who wanted it most on New Year's Eve. They're the ones who made it small enough to do on a bad day, knew what they'd do after a miss, and kept adjusting. You can set all of that up tonight. Write the one resolution, give it a number, write the minimum version under it, and do that minimum once before you go to bed.
